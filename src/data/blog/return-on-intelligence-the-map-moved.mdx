**The convergence described in May is now under way.** AI suppliers are making customers pay more directly for the computing their agents use. Competing models and features bundled into the main assistants are making some products harder to charge for. Investors are starting to question the spending needed to support the whole system. That strengthens the argument in [Breaking Point](/posts/return-on-intelligence-05-breaking-point), although it does not establish that a broad crash is inevitable or imminent.

The tension is becoming easier to see: AI can become more useful while the businesses supplying it face harder economics. Customers want cheaper intelligence. Suppliers need enough income to cover expensive infrastructure. Software companies want to sell features that their customers increasingly find inside ChatGPT, Gemini or an internally generated application.

In May, I argued that these pressures could reinforce one another until investors stopped treating AI exposure as proof of future profits. This follow-up asks how far that process has moved. The clearest changes are in **billing, competition and the market's interpretation of AI spending**. The evidence for a general collapse in SaaS revenue or a systemic financing crisis is much less conclusive.

_A postscript to the May 2026 [Return on Intelligence series](/posts/return-on-intelligence-the-preface). Evidence checked to 26 October 2026. The labels below are my assessments of that evidence, not a financial forecast._

## The convergence map has moved beyond a thought experiment

The original map brought together eight pressures: cheaper models, visible token costs, disappointing enterprise returns, doubts about infrastructure spending, weaker SaaS pricing, vulnerable AI products, financing stress and a change in investor belief. None needed to be fatal on its own. Several arriving together could change how the market valued the first AI winners.

We can now revisit each pressure using actual announcements, price structures, product releases and market reporting. Some mechanisms are observable; some show early signs; others still have strong counter-evidence. A provider adding a competing feature, for example, establishes competitive pressure. It does not establish that a particular startup has failed.

The most important connection is between the first two pressures. Customers have more choices just as the cost of their own usage becomes harder to ignore. A model that was “good enough” yesterday becomes more attractive when the alternative consumes the month's budget in a few long agent sessions.

## 1. The cost of AI work is reaching the customer

**The change in billing is already real.** GitHub's move from premium requests to token-based AI Credits makes the distinction between a short question and a long agent session visible in the bill. Anthropic and OpenAI also expose usage costs through their enterprise terms, allowances and credits. These arrangements differ, but they push customers towards the same question: how much useful work do we get for the money?

### Copilot changed what the subscription buys

[GitHub announced the change on 27 April, effective 1 June 2026](#source-copilot-billing). It said the old premium-request model had become unsustainable as agents ran longer sessions and consumed more compute. Monthly plans would move to credits based on input, output and cached tokens, while annual subscribers followed a separate transition. The base subscription prices were unchanged.

The date matters. The announcement was available when I wrote in May; the billing transition followed afterwards. This was the implementation of an announced change, rather than a prediction made before anyone saw it coming.

The commercial effect still matters. A fixed-looking subscription gives the user less certainty about how much work they can complete when consumption depends on the model, the context and the number of attempts. Planning, testing, review and rework all draw on that budget.

### Claude and OpenAI expose the same cost in different ways

[Claude Enterprise's current pricing](#source-claude-billing) lists a seat charge plus usage at API rates. That differs from a consumer subscription with usage limits. Paying for access does not mean every additional task has already been paid for.

[OpenAI's current documentation](#source-openai-billing) describes shared Work and Codex usage, included allowances and additional credits, with token rates for credit-based plans. API-key billing remains separate. The documentation also warns against using API prices to estimate how much work is included in a subscription.

Neither page, by itself, tells us when every customer moved to those terms. Nor do these arrangements prove that all AI has become more expensive. The price of a unit of intelligence can fall while the total cost of the work attempted with it rises.

An agent can try more approaches, read more files and spend longer checking its result. That can be worth paying for. It also means the bill needs to be compared with the outcome, including the human time required to accept or correct it. [GitHub's September engineering account](#source-github) makes a related point: reducing tokens in one interaction can increase the cost of the task if the agent then has to recover missing information.

This is the “token economics become visible” pressure from May. It is happening in customer billing. Whether it produces better supplier margins depends on how customers respond.

## 2. More model choice and bundled features weaken some moats

**Customers have more alternatives, while the main assistants cover more of the work for which separate products charge.** Together, these changes make it harder to defend a business that mainly sells access to a model or packages one familiar task.

### Kimi, DeepSeek and Mistral widen the choice

These companies did not first enter the market after May. What has changed is their newer models and the range of work they attempt.

[Kimi expanded its offering with K3 during July](#source-kimi); its current documentation reports released weights. Its October K3.1 Pro announcement promises weights for November, so that release is still pending at this article's cut-off. [DeepSeek's September V4.1-Flash release](#source-deepseek) links architectural efficiency with lower API prices. Its current price card also distinguishes cached input, uncached input and peak versus off-peak use.

[Mistral Large 4, nicknamed “Le Chonk”, entered public API preview on 6 October](#source-mistral), with weights promised for the end of the month. That is another available API choice and a planned open-weight release. It does not establish that those weights were downloadable on 26 October.

Open weights allow organisations to obtain a model for deployment under its licence, rather than relying only on its developer's service. They do not remove hardware costs, operating work or licence conditions. Some models are far too large for an ordinary laptop. Provider benchmarks also need testing against the customer's actual tasks.

Even with those limits, choice changes a purchasing decision. A customer can reserve an expensive model for difficult work and use another for routine steps. A competitor need not win every benchmark to put pressure on the leading supplier's price.

### ChatGPT and Gemini are absorbing product categories

[OpenAI's July Work documentation](#source-platform-bundling) describes an assistant that can research, use files and connected apps, and create documents, spreadsheets, presentations and websites. A narrow product selling one of those functions now has to explain why the customer needs another tool.

On [19 August, Google announced Deep Research in Gemini Live](#source-gemini-research) for all Gemini app users, with applicable limits and availability conditions. On [20 October it announced business tools](#source-gemini-business) covering brand assets, connected apps and financial insights. Those functions overlap with categories of standalone AI software.

The change is the customer's default option. Before buying a specialist tool, they can try a capability in the assistant they already use. The extra product can still win on quality, privacy, integration or responsibility for the result. Its case is weaker if its main advantage was being the first interface to a useful model feature.

That is evidence for the replacement mechanism in May's “thin wrappers” pressure. We can see the function being absorbed into a broader product. Establishing an actual startup failure requires evidence about customers and companies, not just a similar feature on an announcement page. Included access also does not mean unlimited free use.

### Cheaper software construction creates another route to competition

[ArtCraft's attempt to reproduce Adobe workflows](#source-lumien) illustrates a different threat: using agents to build an alternative. [PhotoCraft's documentation](#source-photocraft) describes an early-alpha application with command interfaces and tests against real files. I have reviewed the documentation, not independently benchmarked the application.

An incomplete alternative can serve a narrower group of customers or affect their willingness to pay. But a familiar interface does not establish dependable compatibility, maintenance or professional support. Those are part of the product too.

The evidence for broad SaaS decline remains mixed. [SAP's October results](#source-saas-results), for example, showed stronger cloud revenue and backlog. That is a useful check on the claim that AI has already destroyed established software economics. A replaceable feature and a deeply integrated business system face different threats.

## 3. Investors are starting to question the spending

**Market belief is showing strain, although investors have not settled on a single verdict.** They are asking whether infrastructure will earn enough to cover its cost. They can ask that while continuing to believe AI is useful and demand will grow.

[Reuters' 23 October market report](#source-capex-market) describes concern about financing AI investment and scrutiny of future spending. Its [26 October reporting](#source-belief-market) links technology-stock selling with questions about the eventual payoff. These are signs of belief stress, rather than proof of a lasting break.

Calling this “overbuild” needs care. Investors can fear that too much capacity has been committed for future profitable demand even while today's capacity is busy. [Amazon's April shareholder letter](#source-aws-demand) described shortages and unserved demand. That supplier account predates this update, but it helps distinguish present scarcity from future overinvestment.

Capital spending, or capex, therefore no longer serves only as reassurance. The same announcement can prompt questions about financing, utilisation and returns. That change in interpretation is one of the convergence pressures.

### Enterprise returns are part of the uncertainty

[Gartner's October release](#source-roi) reports that 13% of respondents saw significant or transformational AI value. It drew on a May–July survey of 2,002 CIOs and technology executives. This is evidence of a gap between adoption and major business gains. It does not mean the remaining respondents received no benefit, or that 87% of projects failed.

If customers take longer to turn AI into measurable gains, suppliers may wait longer for the income they expected. Usage-based bills also give customers a stronger reason to stop low-value work. Integration and organisational change therefore matter to the financial story.

### Financing determines how a disappointment spreads

The October report [_Brace for Impact_](#source-report) examines how leases, guarantees, debt and private credit connect the buildout to the wider economy. It describes possible paths to losses beyond AI suppliers, while explicitly declining to predict a particular crash sequence or call a crisis inevitable.

A useful technology does not suspend payment deadlines. If expected income arrives late, some businesses may need to refinance or renegotiate before it catches up. That makes the obligations beneath AI important to the convergence argument.

The report's scenarios remain distinct from observed defaults. A guarantee is not a loan, and a future purchase commitment is not an annual expense. We need the terms and counterparties before estimating who would lose money.

## The pressures reinforce one another

A more visible bill encourages a customer to test cheaper models. More competing models make that move practical. Features bundled into a main assistant make another subscription harder to justify. Suppliers then have to earn their return in a market where customers have more ways to reduce spending.

If infrastructure commitments keep growing at the same time, investors have reason to question who will earn enough to pay for them. That questioning can make new finance harder to obtain. Financial trouble can follow even while the technology improves.

There is also a route through the pressure. Lower prices could create enough additional paid demand to improve utilisation. Customers could find work whose gains exceed the full cost. Suppliers could reduce costs faster than prices fall. A fair assessment has to keep that possibility visible.

The public debate reflects the tension. [Ezra Klein's October episode](#source-klein) brings Paul Kedrosky's bubble argument into discussion with Azeem Azhar's more optimistic view. Klein is the host, rather than the author of one position shared by both guests. The full transcript was inaccessible, so I use the publisher's description for that framing.

[Ed Zitron's October writing](#source-zitron) presses on concentrated exposure and continued spending. His [critique of annualised revenue figures](#source-runrate) supplies another useful discipline: an extrapolated run rate and a completed year's revenue are different measurements. [Azhar's earlier essay on funding quality](#source-azhar) distinguishes productive investment from the transfer of financial risk. Neither side is answered simply by showing that a model can do impressive work.

## The ownership map follows from the convergence

The practical question is what remains worth paying for when intelligence is easier to buy and features are easier to reproduce. The May series identified records, permissions, distribution, reliable execution and knowledge of a particular business. These remain possible advantages, rather than guarantees of profit.

An agent that can propose a refund still needs the correct customer record and authority to issue it. [Microsoft's October security approach](#source-microsoft) puts controls outside the model. In software development, [OpenAI's February harness-engineering account](#source-harness) describes the environment and feedback needed to help agents produce usable changes. That work predates May; it helps explain the mechanism rather than proving a new prediction.

For a customer or investor, the test is whether the company provides something that remains valuable when the model changes. A dependable record, a customer relationship or responsibility for an outcome may survive. A thin layer around one model feature may not.

## What would change this assessment?

The financing concern would weaken if customers renewed because measured gains exceeded their full costs, suppliers showed sustainable margins and cash collections, and infrastructure owners met their obligations without repeated rescue financing. Strong demand would matter most when it became dependable income.

The substitution argument would weaken if customers kept paying specialists because compatibility, service and maintenance outweighed the savings from generated alternatives. The value of those obligations may be more durable than the cost of writing code suggests.

My assessment is that **convergence has begun, but its eventual outcome is still open**. Token economics are visible in bills. Competition is visible in available models and bundled features. Belief stress is visible in questions about the return on AI spending. Those observations strengthen the May argument without turning the map into a crash clock.

The question is becoming more urgent: who earns the benefit of cheaper intelligence, and who has already promised to pay for it?
